When you use AI repeatedly, you may notice that it starts to carry context from one conversation into another.

It might remember a project you've been discussing, a decision you made previously, or a change in priorities that came up along the way.

That's the role of Memory.

While Custom Instructions are the context you deliberately give AI upfront, Memory helps AI build an evolving understanding from your interactions over time.

So, how does that actually work?

A common misconception is that AI is retraining itself on you every time you have a conversation.

That's not usually what's happening.

Instead, the AI system maintains a separate layer of context based on your previous interactions.

As you work with AI, a memory process can identify information that may be useful again. For example, it might notice that you're evaluating a new market, that your team has already ruled out one option, or that a decision depends on a particular stakeholder.

It can then organize that information into memory and update it as new information appears.

So, you can think of the process like this:

You interact. AI identifies useful context. Memory evolves. And relevant memory is brought into future conversations.

Importantly, AI doesn't need to bring everything it remembers into every conversation. It can retrieve the context that appears relevant to what you're asking now.

Over time, this creates an evolving working context around you and your work.

So why does that matter?

The biggest benefit is continuity.

Imagine you've been discussing a market expansion across several conversations. You've explored different options, ruled some out, and discussed new constraints.

Later, you might simply ask, "What should we do next?"

Memory can help AI understand what has already happened instead of treating that question as a completely new problem.

But that continuity also creates a risk.

Memory can become a persistent assumption.

Imagine AI remembers that cost reduction is your priority. Three months later, your strategy changes to growth.

If the old memory continues to influence its answers, the response may still feel personalized, but it's personalized to outdated context.

That's why an important principle is:

Memory can make AI more relevant. It does not automatically make AI more correct.

So there are three habits worth building.

First, pay attention to what AI is carrying forward. When it references previous decisions, projects, or priorities, check whether they're still accurate.

Second, correct it when things change. Treat memory as evolving context, not a permanent record.

And third, know when you want a fresh start. For a one-off task, a sensitive question, or when you want an independent perspective, you may want to use a temporary or non-personalized conversation where the platform supports it.

The distinction to remember is simple:

Custom Instructions are what you intentionally tell AI about how to work with you.

Memory is what AI learns and carries forward from working with you over time.

One gives AI a starting point.

The other builds continuity as you work together.
