Ask executives whether their people feel well informed about the company's AI strategy, and eighty percent say yes.

But ask employees themselves, and only thirty percent agree. That's according to research from Columbia Business School and BCG.

And if we can't even communicate change effectively, how can we expect to make it happen?

One company that has made real progress is Zapier, where almost everyone now uses AI every day.

When we interviewed Chief People Officer Brandon Sammut, he explained how Zapier combined four things: leadership commitment, hands-on experimentation, internal champions, and redesigned workflows.

The results speak for themselves. Their support team cut average ticket handling time in half, while maintaining customer satisfaction.

So what can we learn from companies like Zapier about making AI transformation stick?

[pause] First, start by listening. Understand where your people are before deciding what they need.

How are they using AI today? What skills do they have? Which workflows frustrate them? And what worries them?

Our transformation partner, Phil Kirschner, uses a framework called the Four Forces.

People move toward change when they're frustrated with today's work, and excited about a better future. But anxiety, and established habits, hold them back.

Employee listening shows you what's moving people forward, and what's holding them back.

Use interviews, surveys, and conversations about real work. And keep listening as the transformation progresses.

[pause] Second, build a network of AI Champions, with a structured program behind it.

Champions are employees with proven AI skills. They help colleagues learn, redesign workflows, and spot new opportunities.

At Citi, more than four thousand AI Accelerators support adoption across the organization. PwC Netherlands built a similar network reaching thousands of employees, and used people analytics to find them.

But appointing Champions isn't enough. Give them advanced training, dedicated time, and real responsibility for supporting their teams.

[pause] Third, let employees shape the change. Involve them in building and testing the solutions that will change their work.

At Arizona State University, faculty and staff proposed their own AI applications, and around two hundred projects went live within eight months.

At Kaiser Permanente, clinicians helped test AI documentation tools before they were rolled out more widely.

Managers are essential here. They need to make time for experimentation, remove obstacles, and address concerns about how roles will evolve.

[pause] Finally, measure progress, and keep adapting. Track both employee progress and business outcomes.

Are capabilities improving? Are concerns being addressed? Are people adopting the redesigned workflows?

And are those workflows delivering better quality, faster service, and measurable value?

Use regular employee listening, and feedback from your Champions, to understand what needs to change.

Because unlike a traditional software rollout, AI transformation has no fixed finish line.

The goal is to build an organization where people have the confidence, the capabilities, and the support to keep improving how they work with AI. Together.
